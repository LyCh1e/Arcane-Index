# Behavioural Ethics and Workplace Ethics

## Behavioural Ethics

**Behavioural ethics** is a social-scientific field that studies how people **actually behave** when confronted with ethical dilemmas — as opposed to *normative ethics*, which prescribes how people *ought* to behave.

- **Normative ethics** (Kantianism, Utilitarianism, etc.) → prescriptive ("what *should* you do?")
- **Behavioural ethics** → descriptive ("what *do* people actually do, and why?")

---

## Situational and Social Factors

Several external factors influence ethical behaviour independently of personal moral beliefs.

### Obedience to Authority

People tend to obey authority figures even when doing so conflicts with their own moral judgement.

**Milgram Experiment (1961–1962):** Stanley Milgram demonstrated that ordinary people would administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to strangers simply because an authority figure (the experimenter) instructed them to. Most participants continued to the maximum shock level when instructed.

**Implication for IT professionals:** Employees may act unethically if ordered to by a manager. A key professional responsibility is to speak up when professional judgement conflicts with instructions from authority.

### Roles

The roles we occupy can significantly alter our behaviour, independently of personality.

**Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971):** Participants randomly assigned to the role of "guards" in a simulated prison began acting in authoritarian, often abusive ways toward those assigned as "prisoners." The experiment had to be terminated early.

**Implication:** People may behave in ways inconsistent with their personal ethics when they occupy a certain role (employee, manager, soldier).

### Moral Licensing

Previous ethical behaviour may lead individuals to feel morally licensed to engage in subsequent unethical behaviour — a "moral credit" that they feel permits a later lapse.

*Example:* A person who donated to charity last week may feel justified in a small act of dishonesty.

### Anonymity and Likelihood of Getting Caught

- The less likely someone is to be identified or caught, the more likely they are to behave unethically.
- **Online anonymity** reduces the perceived likelihood of getting caught, lowering inhibitions against unethical behaviour.
- **CCTV** is an effective deterrent precisely because it increases the perceived likelihood of detection.

## Cognitive Biases Affecting Ethical Behaviour

**Cognitive biases** are systematic errors in thinking that affect decisions and judgements.

### Cognitive Dissonance

**Definition:** The mental discomfort experienced when a person holds two or more contradictory beliefs, or when actions conflict with beliefs.

**Defence mechanisms:**
- Changing the behaviour to align with the belief.
- Changing the belief to align with the behaviour.
- Avoiding or denying the conflicting cognition.
- **Rationalisation** — constructing a post-hoc justification for the inconsistency.

*Example:* A person who values honesty but lies to their boss may rationalise: "It's just a small lie, and it prevented a bigger problem."

### Confirmation Bias

**Definition:** The tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports prior beliefs.

- People tend to ignore or discount evidence that contradicts their existing views.
- *Example:* A manager who believes an employee underperforms will notice and remember instances of poor performance while overlooking good ones.
- **Implication:** Can lead to poor ethical decision-making by filtering out contradictory ethical considerations.

### Conformity Bias

**Definition:** The tendency to align behaviour with what others do or what the group norm is, rather than following independent judgement.

- Can motivate prosocial behaviour (e.g., donating to charity when others do).
- Can also lead people to commit acts against their values to gain social acceptance.
- *Example:* An employee who witnesses colleagues falsifying reports may go along with it to fit in.

### Fundamental Attribution Error and Self-Serving Bias

**Fundamental Attribution Error:** When explaining *other people's* behaviour, we overestimate the role of character/personality and underestimate situational factors.

- *Example:* "My colleague is rude to customers" (personality attribution) rather than "they must be having a difficult day" (situational attribution).

**Self-Serving Bias:** The opposite error applied to *oneself* — attributing one's own successes to personal character (internal) and failures to external circumstances.

- *Example:* "I got promoted because I am talented. I missed the deadline because the project scope kept changing."

## Workplace Ethics

### Conflict of Interest

A **conflict of interest** arises when a person's private interests (personal, financial, or professional) could improperly influence their professional judgement or actions — even if the influence does not actually occur.

**Examples:**

| Type | Example |
|------|---------|
| **Financial** | Financial adviser recommending investments in a company they personally hold shares in |
| **Personal relationship** | Manager promoting a close friend without considering qualifications |
| **Dual role** | Researcher studying a product while employed by the product's manufacturer |

**Managing conflicts of interest:**
- **Disclose** the conflict to all relevant parties.
- **Recuse** yourself from decision-making in affected areas.
- **Adhere** to institutional policies for managing conflicts.

---

### Bribery vs. Small Gifts

The distinction between a bribe and an acceptable gift can be culturally dependent. The key criterion is whether the gift is **intended to improperly influence** a professional decision. When in doubt, disclose.

---

### Whistleblowing

**Whistleblowing** is the act of reporting illegal or unethical activity within an organisation to internal or external authorities.

In professional ethics, whistleblowing is considered only with a **good motive** — the genuine desire to help the public or prevent harm (not retaliation or personal advantage).

**Conditions for ethically justified whistleblowing:**

| Condition | Requirement |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Serious harm** | The organisation's action could cause serious harm to the public or employees |
| **Internal reporting** | Concern has been reported to the immediate supervisor, who has not acted |
| **Exhausted internal channels** | All internal channels (reporting chain, ethics hotlines) have been tried and failed |
| **Evidence** | Documented evidence sufficient to convince a reasonable person |
| **Good faith belief** | The employee genuinely believes exposure will prevent the harm |

**Competing views:** Some argue whistleblowing is a betrayal of employer loyalty. The dominant professional ethics view is that the duty to the public **overrides** loyalty to the organisation when all five conditions above are satisfied, and that properly motivated, documented whistleblowing is not only ethically justified but sometimes obligatory.

---

### Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR)

**CSR:** The concept that an organisation should act ethically by taking responsibility for the impact of its actions on:
- The **environment**.
- The **community**.
- The **welfare of its employees**.

**Elements of an ethical workplace culture:**
- Clear ethical codes and standards.
- Training programmes for all employees.
- Confidential reporting mechanisms (ethics hotlines).
- Consistent enforcement.
- **Leadership commitment** — leaders must visibly demonstrate and prioritise ethical behaviour; they set the example and must allocate resources to ethics programmes.
