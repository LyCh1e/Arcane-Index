# Ethical Frameworks

## What Is Ethics?

Ethics focuses on people's **voluntary, moral choices**. It involves **rational, systematic analysis** — conclusions should be supported by explanations based on facts, shared values, and logic. A good ethical theory produces **persuasive, logical arguments** that a sceptical yet open-minded audience would find compelling.

---

## Three Levels of Moral Guidance

| Level | Description | Generality |
|-------|-------------|-----------|
| **Rules** | Specific, context-bound (e.g., "no eating in the classroom") | Lowest |
| **Principles** | More general (e.g., professional codes of ethics) | Medium |
| **Ethical frameworks/theories** | Most general; used when rules/principles do not apply | Highest |

Ethical frameworks are essential in novel situations where no existing rule or principle applies.

## Non-Workable Theories

The following four frameworks are examined but **rejected** as workable bases for professional ethical decision-making:

### Subjective Relativism

Each person decides right and wrong for themselves. "What's right for you may not be right for me."

- **Case for:** Well-meaning people disagree on moral issues.
- **Case against:** Blurs doing what you *think* is right with doing what you *feel* like doing; decisions need not be based on reason.
- ❌ **Not workable.**

### Cultural Relativism

Right and wrong are determined by a society's moral guidelines, which vary across time and place.

- **Case for:** Different social contexts demand different moral guidelines; one society should not judge another.
- **Case against:** That societies *differ* does not mean they *ought* to differ; provides no way to resolve cross-cultural conflicts; societies actually share many core values.
- ❌ **Not workable.**

### Divine Command Theory

Good actions align with God's will; bad actions contradict it.

- **Case for:** God is all-knowing and all-good.
- **Case against:** Society is multicultural and secular; holy books disagree; many modern moral problems are not addressed in scripture; based on obedience rather than reason.
- ❌ **Not workable (for secular, professional purposes).**

### Ethical Egoism

Each person should act exclusively in their own long-term self-interest.

- **Case for:** Practical; community may benefit from individual self-interest (laissez-faire argument).
- **Case against:** May justify clearly immoral acts (e.g., refusing to rescue a drowning child when no one is watching).
- ❌ **Not workable.**

## Workable Ethical Theories

### Kantianism

**Proposed by:** Immanuel Kant

**Core idea:** Moral principles can be derived from pure reason. An action's moral worth is judged by the underlying principle on which it is performed.

**Deontological:** Focuses on whether the action itself is right or wrong, not on its consequences.

**The Categorical Imperative** — an absolute, unconditional requirement that must be obeyed in all circumstances:

#### 1st Formulation — Universality

> "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."

**Application:** Ask whether the principle behind your action can be consistently universalised. If universalising it creates a contradiction, the action is unethical.

*Example 1 — Borrowing money without intending to repay:*
- Maxim: "I will borrow money without intending to repay it."
- Universalised: no one would lend money anymore — the practice of borrowing becomes self-defeating.
- **Verdict: Wrong.**

*Example 2 — Submitting a purchased report as one's own work:*
- Maxim: "You may claim credit for work performed by someone else."
- Universalised: reports would no longer be credible indicators of knowledge; professors would not accept them.
- **Verdict: Wrong.**

#### 2nd Formulation — Humanity

> "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."

*Example:* Submitting a purchased report deceives the professor — treating the professor as a means (instrument for passing the course) rather than as an end in themselves. **Verdict: Wrong.**

**Strengths:** Holds everyone to the same standard; produces universal moral guidelines.
**Weaknesses:** Sometimes no rule adequately characterises an action; conflicting principles can be difficult to resolve.
✅ **Workable (with limitations).**

---

### Utilitarianism

**Proposed by:** Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill

**Consequentialist:** The morality of an action is judged entirely by its consequences.

- **Utility** = happiness = advantage = benefit = pleasure.
- **Unhappiness** = cost = pain.

**Principle of Utility (Greatest Happiness Principle):**
> An action is right to the extent that it increases the total happiness of all affected parties; wrong to the extent that it decreases it.

#### Act Utilitarianism

For each action, sum the changes in happiness of all affected parties:
- If Sum > 0: the action is good.
- If Sum < 0: the action is bad.
- The *right* action is the one that **maximises** the total sum.

*Example — Highway routing:* A new road 1 mile shorter benefits 15,000 daily commuters but displaces 150 households and destroys wildlife habitat. Act utilitarianism would weigh all benefits (time × commuters × days) against harms (displaced residents, habitat loss) to determine net utility.

#### Rule Utilitarianism

Rather than evaluating each individual action, evaluate **moral rules**: adopt those rules which, if followed universally, would maximise total happiness.

**Strengths:** Practical; focuses on the well-being of all affected parties.
**Weaknesses:** Can justify punishing the innocent if the numbers work out; consequences are difficult to measure or predict with certainty.
✅ **Workable (with limitations).**

### Social Contract Theory

**Key contributors:** Thomas Hobbes (1651), John Locke (1689), Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762), John Rawls (20th century revival)

**Core idea:** Morality consists of rules governing how people treat one another that **rational people would agree to accept for their mutual benefit**, on the condition that others follow those rules too.

**John Rawls' Principles of Justice as Fairness:**

*First Principle (Equal Liberties):* Each person has the same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, compatible with the same scheme for all.

*Second Principle:* Social and economic inequalities are permissible only if:
1. Attached to offices and positions open to all under fair equality of opportunity.
2. To the greatest benefit of the **least-advantaged** members of society (**the difference principle**).

**The Veil of Ignorance:** Rawls proposed that just principles should be chosen from behind a hypothetical "veil of ignorance" — a position in which people do not know their place in society, class, wealth, intelligence, or conception of the good. This forces impartial reasoning.

**Strengths:** Framed around rights and fairness, not just consequences; provides a basis for rights that cannot be overridden by aggregate utility.
**Weaknesses:** Difficult to determine what people would truly agree to from behind the veil; does not adequately address duties to animals or those unable to enter contracts.
✅ **Workable (with limitations).**

---

### Virtue Ethics

**Proposed by:** Aristotle

**Core idea:** Rather than asking "what rule should I follow?" or "what consequences should I maximise?", virtue ethics focuses on the **character of the moral agent**. Virtues allow human beings to flourish and be truly happy.

**Two types of virtue:**
- **Intellectual virtues:** associated with reasoning and truth.
- **Moral virtues:** virtues of character — honesty, courage, generosity, integrity. Developed by habitually performing and *feeling* the right way.

**Moral guidance:**
1. Identify the relevant virtues for the situation.
2. Ask: "What would a person with these virtues do?"
3. Also consider the specific circumstances.

**Strengths:** Focuses on who we are, not just what we do; emphasises moral development and character.
**Weaknesses:** Does not always give clear guidance in specific situations; cultures may identify different virtues.
✅ **Workable (with limitations).**

## Framework Summary

| Framework | Core Idea | Workable? |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Subjective Relativism | Each person decides for themselves | ❌ |
| Cultural Relativism | Society determines right/wrong | ❌ |
| Divine Command Theory | God's will determines right/wrong | ❌ |
| Ethical Egoism | Act in your own self-interest | ❌ |
| Kantianism | Act on universalisable principles; treat persons as ends | ✅ |
| Act Utilitarianism | Maximise overall happiness per action | ✅ |
| Rule Utilitarianism | Adopt rules that maximise happiness if universally followed | ✅ |
| Social Contract Theory | Follow rules rational people would mutually agree to | ✅ |
| Virtue Ethics | Act as a virtuous person would act | ✅ |

## Professional Codes of Ethics

### IT Professional Skills (Three Categories)

1. **Information management and technology skills** — core technical competence.
2. **Soft skills** — interpersonal communication, teamwork, leadership, business and project management.
3. **Commitment to ethical conduct** — honesty, integrity, fairness; adherence to professional codes.

### SCS Code of Conduct (Singapore Computer Society)

Four pillars:

| Pillar | Key Duties |
|--------|-----------|
| **Integrity** | Do not claim unearned competence; maintain confidentiality; be impartial; give credit to others |
| **Full Responsibility** | Carry out assignments professionally; adhere to client standards; inform employer of consequences if overruled |
| **Competence** | Continuously upgrade knowledge and skills; support others' professional development; promote accurate public understanding of IT |
| **Professionalism** | Uphold professional standards; do not slander colleagues; use skills for human welfare |

### ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct

Four sections:

1. **General Ethical Principles** — contribute to human well-being; avoid harm; be honest; be fair; respect IP; respect privacy; honour confidentiality.
2. **Professional Responsibilities** — achieve high quality; maintain competence; know applicable rules; accept and provide peer review; evaluate systems and risks comprehensively; work only in areas of competence; foster public understanding; access resources only when authorised; design secure systems.
3. **Professional Leadership Principles** — ensure public good is the central concern; support social responsibilities; manage resources to enhance quality of working life; create opportunities for professional growth; use care when retiring systems; give special care to systems integrated into society's infrastructure.
4. **Compliance** — uphold and promote the Code; treat violations as inconsistent with ACM membership.
