### 1.1 The Information Age

The **Information Age** is characterised by unprecedented access to information in the modern era.

**Catalysts for the Information Age:**
- Low-cost computers
- High-speed communication networks

**New technologies that continue to emerge:** smartphones, low-cost drones, self-driving cars.

**Our Relationship with Technology:**
- There is a dynamic between people and technology: people create and adopt technology, and once adopted, technology changes society.
- Using technology can change people — experiences cause physical changes in the brain (e.g., receiving information releases dopamine, producing a desire to seek additional information). Technology use can also have psychological effects such as dependency on cell phones.
- Technologies solve problems but may create new ones:
  - **Automobiles** → greater mobility, but also traffic jams.
  - **The Web** → supports valuable information retrieval tools, but children may be exposed to inappropriate content.
  - **Low-cost international communication** → global access to news and entertainment, but jobs can be outsourced to less expensive labour markets.
  - **Mechanical calculators** historically led to "de-skilling" and the "feminisation" of bookkeeping.

### 1.3 Guidance from Rules, Ethical Principles, and Ethical Frameworks

The three levels of moral guidance, from most specific to most general:

| Level | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Rules** | Very specific, easy to apply, applicable only to specific contexts | "No eating or drinking in the classroom" |
| **Principles** | More general than rules | Professional codes of ethics |
| **Ethical Theories / Frameworks** | Most general; important when rules/principles don't apply | Kantianism, Utilitarianism |

Ethical frameworks are important in situations that have not been studied or encountered previously, or where rules and principles are not applicable.

### 1.4 Code of Ethics / Conduct

- Most professionals are subject to strict **codes of conduct** that enshrine rigorous ethical and moral obligations.
- Professional standards of practice and ethics for a particular field are typically agreed upon and maintained through widely recognised professional associations, such as **IEEE** and **ACM**.
- Ethics is often studied as part of professional training (accounting, law, medicine all require it).

**Definitions:**
- **Profession:** A vocation founded upon specialised educational training, the purpose of which is to supply disinterested objective counsel and service to others, for a direct and definite compensation.
- **Professional:** A member of a profession, or any person who works in a specified professional activity. The term also describes the standards of education and training that prepare members of the profession with the particular knowledge and skills necessary to perform their specific role. In some cultures, refers to a social stratum of well-educated workers who enjoy considerable work autonomy and engage in creative and intellectually challenging work.

### 1.5 SCS Code of Conduct (Singapore Computer Society)

SCS members will:
1. Act at all times with **integrity**
2. Accept **full responsibility** for their work
3. Always aim to increase their **competence**
4. Act with **professionalism** to enhance the prestige of the profession and the Society

**SCS — Integrity:** Members will:
- Not lay claim to a level of competence that they do not possess
- Act with complete discretion when entrusted with confidential information
- Be impartial when giving advice and disclose any relevant personal interests
- Give credit for work done by others where credit is due

**SCS — Full Responsibility:** Members will:
- Carry out their assignments in a professional manner
- Adhere to their employers' or clients' standards and guidelines
- Indicate to their employers or clients the consequences to be expected if their professional judgement is overruled

**SCS — Competence:** Members will:
- Continue to upgrade their knowledge and skills, and be aware of relevant development in the technology they are involved in
- Provide opportunity and encouragement for professional development and advancement to fellow professionals and aspirants to the profession
- Extend public knowledge, understanding and appreciation of information technology, and oppose false or deceptive statements related to information technology of which they are aware

**SCS — Professionalism:** Members will:
- Uphold and improve the professional standards of the Society through participation in their formulation, establishment and enforcement
- Not seek personal advantage to the detriment of the Society
- Not speak on behalf of the Society without proper authority
- Not slander the professional reputation of any other person
- Use their special knowledge and skill for the advancement of human welfare

## Lecture 2 — Ethical Frameworks {#lecture-2}

### 2.1 What is Ethics?

- Ethics focuses on people's **voluntary, moral choices**.
- Ethics involves **rational, systematic analysis** — conclusions should be supported by explanations based on facts, shared values, and logic.
- A good ethical theory enables you to make **persuasive, logical arguments** — it produces explanations that are persuasive to a skeptical yet open-minded audience.

---

### 2.2 Subjective Relativism

**Definition:** Each person decides right and wrong for himself or herself. "What's right for you may not be right for me." There are no universal standards of right and wrong.

**Case for Subjective Relativism:**
- Well-meaning and intelligent people disagree on moral issues.

**Case Against Subjective Relativism:**
- Blurs the line between doing what you think is right and doing what you feel like doing.
- Decisions may not be based on reason.
- **Conclusion: NOT a workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.3 Cultural Relativism

**Definition:** What is "right" and "wrong" depends upon a society's moral guidelines. These guidelines vary from place to place and from time to time. A particular action may be right in one society at one time and wrong in another society or at another time.

**Ethical Dilemma Example (Baumhart Study):** You are riding in a car driven by a close friend who hits a pedestrian while going 35 mph in a 20 mph zone. His lawyer says if you testify he was going 20 mph, you will save him from serious consequences. Results showed that 90% of Norwegians would not testify to the lower speed, compared to about 10% of Yugoslavians. About 3/4 of Americans and Canadians agreed with the dominant Norwegian view, while Mexicans were fairly evenly divided — demonstrating that cultures differ on this.

**Case for Cultural Relativism:**
- Different social contexts demand different moral guidelines.
- It is arrogant for one society to judge another.

**Case Against Cultural Relativism:**
- Because two societies have different moral views doesn't mean they *ought* to have different views. It provides no way out for cultures in conflict.
- Societies do, in fact, share certain core values. It doesn't explain how moral guidelines are determined.
- **Conclusion: NOT a workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.4 Divine Command Theory

**Definition:** Good actions are those aligned with God's will; bad actions are those contrary to God's will. Holy books reveal God's will and should be used as moral decision-making guides.

**Case for Divine Command Theory:**
- God is all-good and all-knowing. God is the ultimate authority. We owe obedience to our Creator.

**Case Against Divine Command Theory:**
- Society is increasingly multicultural and secular.
- Different holy books disagree on certain teachings.
- Some modern moral problems are not directly addressed in scripture.
- Based on obedience, not reason.
- **Conclusion: NOT a workable ethical theory for our purposes.**

---

### 2.5 Ethical Egoism

**Definition:** Each person should focus exclusively on his or her self-interest. An action that provides the person with maximum long-term benefit is the morally right action; otherwise, it is morally wrong.

**Case for Ethical Egoism:**
- It is practical since we are already inclined to do what's best for ourselves.
- It is better to let other people take care of themselves.
- The community can benefit when individuals put their well-being first (aligned with laissez-faire economic theories).

**Case Against Ethical Egoism:**
- It may justify immoral behaviour — e.g., not saving a drowning child when no one is watching.
- **Conclusion: NOT a workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.6 Kantianism

**Definition:** Moral principles can be derived from pure reason. An action is performed in accordance with some principle; the moral worth of the action is judged by this underlying principle.

Proposed by **Immanuel Kant**.

**The Categorical Imperative:** Central to Kantian philosophy. A categorical imperative denotes an absolute, unconditional requirement that must be obeyed in all circumstances (contrast with a *hypothetical imperative*, which applies to someone who wishes to attain certain ends, e.g., "I must study to pass the exam").

**1st Formulation — Universality:**
> "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."

*Example 1:* Borrowing money with no intention to repay. Maxim: "I will borrow money without the intention to pay it back." If universalised, no person would lend money anymore — a contradiction. Therefore, this action violates the categorical imperative and is **unethical**.

*Example 2:* Carla (a single mother taking evening courses) purchases a report and submits it as her own work. Maxim: "You may claim credit for work performed by someone else." If universalised, reports would no longer be a credible indicator of students' knowledge, and professors would not give credit for reports — self-defeating. Therefore, it is **wrong** for Carla to turn in a purchased report.

**2nd Formulation — Humanity:**
> "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."

*Example (Carla):* By submitting a purchased report, Carla attempts to deceive the professor — treating the professor as a means (passing the course) rather than an end. Therefore, wrong.

**Case for Kantianism:**
- Holds everyone to the same standard.
- Produces universal moral guidelines.

**Case Against Kantianism:**
- Sometimes no rule adequately characterises an action.
- Sometimes difficult to resolve conflicting underlying principles.
- **Conclusion: Despite weaknesses, a workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.7 & 2.8 Utilitarianism

**Background — Consequentialism:**
- **Deontology** (Divine Command Theory, Kantianism): The morality of an action should be based on whether the action itself is right or wrong under a series of rules and principles, rather than based on consequences.
- **Consequentialism:** The consequences of one's conduct are the ultimate basis for judgement about the rightness or wrongness of that conduct.
- Utilitarianism falls under consequentialism.

**Utility:**
- Proposed by English philosophers **Jeremy Bentham** and **John Stuart Mill**.
- An action is good if its benefits exceed its harms; bad if harms exceed benefits.
- **Utility** = a measure of happiness.
- Happiness = advantage = benefit = good = pleasure.
- Unhappiness = disadvantage = cost = evil = pain.

**Principle of Utility (Greatest Happiness Principle):**
> An action is right (or wrong) to the extent that it increases (or decreases) the total happiness of the affected parties.

**Act Utilitarianism:**
- Add up the change in happiness of all beings affected by the action.
- Happiness is a positive number; unhappiness is a negative number.
- If Sum > 0, action is good; if Sum < 0, action is bad.
- The right action to take is the one that **maximises the sum**.

*Example — Highway Routing Scenario:* A state is considering a new highway that is 1 mile shorter, but would require removing 150 houses and destroying some wildlife habitat; 15,000 people would use it every weekday. An act utilitarian analysis would weigh all the benefits (time saved × 15,000 commuters × working days) against the harms (displaced residents, wildlife loss) to determine whether the net utility is positive.

**Rule Utilitarianism:**
- We ought to adopt moral rules which, if followed by everyone, will lead to the greatest increase in total happiness.
- Act utilitarianism applies the principle of utility to individual actions; rule utilitarianism applies the principle of utility to **moral rules**.

**Case for Utilitarianism:**
- Focuses on happiness and well-being of all affected.
- Practical decision-making guide for policies.

**Case Against Utilitarianism:**
- Can justify actions that seem morally wrong if they produce higher overall utility (e.g., punishing the innocent).
- Difficult to predict or measure all consequences.
- **Conclusion: A workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.9 Social Contract Theory

**Definition:** Morality consists of a set of rules governing how people are to treat one another that rational people will agree to accept for their mutual benefit, on the condition that others follow those rules too.

**Key contributors:** Thomas Hobbes (1651), John Locke (1689), Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1762). More recently, **John Rawls** revived interest in social contract theory.

Rawls asked: "Can society be organised around fair principles of cooperation in a way the people would stably accept?"

**John Rawls' Principles of Justice as Fairness:**

*First Principle (Equal Liberties):* Each person has the same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties, which scheme is compatible with the same scheme of liberties for all.

*Second Principle:* Social and economic inequalities are to satisfy two conditions:
- They are to be attached to offices and positions open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity.
- They are to be the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society (**the difference principle**).

**The Veil of Ignorance:** Rawls proposed that principles of justice should be chosen from behind a "veil of ignorance" — a hypothetical position in which people do not know their place in society, class position, fortune in the distribution of assets and abilities, intelligence, or even their conception of the good.

**Case for Social Contract Theory:**
- Framed around rights and fairness, not just consequences.
- Provides a basis for rights that cannot be overridden simply for social utility.

**Case Against Social Contract Theory:**
- Difficult to determine what people would agree to from behind the veil of ignorance.
- Does not adequately address duties to non-human animals or those unable to enter contracts.
- **Conclusion: A workable ethical theory.**

---

### 2.10 Virtue Ethics

**Definition:** Rather than focusing on what rules to follow or what consequences to maximise, virtue ethics focuses on the character of the moral agent.

**Background:** Proposed by **Aristotle**. Virtues allow human beings to flourish and be truly happy.

**Two types of virtue:**
- **Intellectual virtues:** virtues associated with reasoning and truth.
- **Moral virtues:** virtues of character (e.g., honesty, courage, generosity). These are developed by habitually performing the right action — a disposition to act in a certain way *and* feel in a certain way.

**Moral guidance from Virtue Ethics:**
For a given ethical problem:
1. Identify the relevant virtues.
2. The right action is the one consistent with these virtues — "What would a person with these virtues do?"
3. Also consider the circumstances of the situation.

**Case for Virtue Ethics:**
- Focuses on who we are, not just what we do.
- Emphasises moral development and character.

**Case Against Virtue Ethics:**
- Does not always give clear guidance on what to do in specific situations.
- Different cultures may identify different virtues.
- **Conclusion: A workable ethical theory.**

## Lecture 3 — Behavioural Ethics {#lecture-3}

### 3.1 What is Behavioural Ethics?

**Behavioural ethics** is a new field of social scientific research that seeks to understand how people **actually behave** when confronted with ethical dilemmas.

While **normative ethics** (Kantianism, Utilitarianism, Social Contract Theory, Virtue Ethics) provides frameworks that prescribe how people *ought* to act, behavioural ethics is **descriptive** in nature. It seeks to describe why people make the ethical and unethical decisions that they do.

**Overview of Lecture 3:**
1. What is behavioural ethics?
2. Situational and social factors affecting ethical behaviour.
3. Cognitive biases affecting ethical behaviour.

---

### 3.2 Situational and Social Factors

Several situational and social factors influence whether people act ethically or unethically, regardless of their beliefs.

#### 3.2.1 Obedience to Authority

People tend to obey authority figures even when doing so conflicts with their own moral judgement.

- **Milgram Experiment (1961–1962):** Stanley Milgram's famous experiment showed that ordinary people would administer what they believed were dangerous electric shocks to strangers simply because an authority figure (the experimenter) instructed them to. Most participants continued to the maximum level when instructed by the authority figure. This demonstrated that situational factors — specifically the presence of authority — can override personal moral beliefs.
- **Implications for IT professionals:** Employees may act unethically if ordered to by a manager. A key professional responsibility is to speak up when professional judgement is at odds with instructions from authority.

#### 3.2.2 Roles

The roles we occupy can significantly influence our behaviour.

- **Stanford Prison Experiment (Zimbardo, 1971):** Participants randomly assigned to the role of "guards" in a simulated prison environment began to act in authoritarian, often abusive ways toward those assigned as "prisoners." The experiment had to be terminated early. This demonstrated that roles — not personality — can drive behaviour.
- **Implications:** People may behave in ways inconsistent with their personal ethics when they adopt a certain role (e.g., an employee, a soldier, a manager).

#### 3.2.3 Moral Licensing

Previous acts of ethical behaviour may lead individuals to feel morally licensed to engage in subsequent unethical behaviour. In other words, doing something good gives a person a "moral credit" that they may then use to justify a later lapse.

- *Example:* A person who donated to charity last week may feel justified in a small act of dishonesty.

#### 3.2.4 Anonymity and Likelihood of Getting Caught

- The less likely someone is to be identified or caught, the more likely they are to behave unethically.
- **Anonymity** (e.g., online environments) reduces the perceived likelihood of getting caught and can lower inhibitions against unethical behaviour.
- **Likelihood of getting caught:** CCTV increases the likelihood of getting caught and is therefore often used to deter crime. Former burglars found CCTV to be the most effective deterrent for home burglaries and car thefts.

---

### 3.3 Cognitive Biases Affecting Ethical Behaviour

**Cognitive biases** are systematic errors in thinking that affect the decisions and judgements people make.

**Key cognitive biases covered:**
1. Cognitive dissonance
2. Confirmation bias
3. Conformity bias
4. Fundamental attribution error and self-serving bias

#### 3.3.1 Cognitive Dissonance

**Cognitive dissonance** is the mental discomfort experienced when a person holds two or more contradictory beliefs, or when actions conflict with beliefs.

**Typical defence mechanisms against cognitive dissonance:**
- Changing the behaviour.
- Changing the unwelcome cognition (updating the belief).
- Avoiding or denying unwelcome cognition.
- Explaining away the unwelcome thoughts by **rationalization** — constructing a post-hoc justification.

*Example:* A person who believes honesty is important but lies to their boss may rationalize: "It's just a small lie, and it prevented a bigger problem."

#### 3.3.2 Confirmation Bias

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to search for, interpret, favour, and recall information in a way that confirms or supports one's prior beliefs or values.

- People tend to ignore or discount evidence that contradicts their pre-existing beliefs.
- *Example:* A manager who believes an employee is underperforming will notice and remember instances of poor performance while ignoring instances of good performance.
- **Implication:** Can lead to poor ethical decision-making by filtering out contradictory ethical considerations.

#### 3.3.3 Conformity Bias

**Conformity bias** is the tendency to behave in line with what others do or what the group norm is, rather than following one's own independent judgement.

- Conformity bias may motivate people to act altruistically (e.g., donating to charity when they see others doing it).
- Conformity bias may also lead people to commit acts against their own values in order to gain social acceptance.
- *Example:* An employee who witnesses colleagues falsifying reports may go along with it to fit in with the group.

#### 3.3.4 Fundamental Attribution Error and Self-Serving Bias

**Fundamental Attribution Error:** The tendency to overestimate the role of character/personality and underestimate the role of situational factors when explaining other people's behaviour.

- *Example:* If a colleague is rude to a customer, we tend to think "he is a rude person" rather than "he must be having a bad day."

**Self-Serving Bias:** The opposite error applied to oneself — the tendency to attribute one's own successes to personal character (internal) and failures to situational factors (external).

- *Example:* "I got the promotion because I am talented. I missed the deadline because the project scope kept changing."

## Lecture 4 — Workplace Ethics {#lecture-4}

### 4.1 Conflict of Interest

A **conflict of interest** arises when a person's private interests (personal, financial, or professional) could improperly influence their professional judgement or actions, even if it does not actually do so.

**Examples:**
- **Financial conflict of interest:** A financial advisor recommending investments in a company they personally own stocks in, potentially prioritising their own financial gain over the client's best interests.
- **Personal relationship conflict of interest:** A manager promoting a close friend or family member without considering their qualifications or suitability for the position.
- **Dual role conflict of interest:** A researcher conducting a study on a product while also being employed by the manufacturer, potentially influencing research findings to favour their employer's interests.

**Managing conflicts of interest:**
- Disclose the conflict.
- Recuse yourself from decision-making in areas where the conflict exists.
- Adhere to institutional policies for managing conflicts.

---

### 4.2 Bribery vs. Small Gifts

The line between a bribe and an acceptable gift can be culturally dependent. Different cultures may have different interpretations of the purpose of a small gift. The key distinction is whether the gift is intended to improperly influence a professional decision.

---

### 4.3 Whistleblowing

**Whistleblowing** is the act of reporting illegal or unethical activity within an organisation to internal or external authorities.

**Motives for whistleblowing:**
- **Good motive:** Desire to help the public or prevent harm.
- **Questionable motives:** Retaliation, avoiding personal punishment.

In this course, we consider whistleblowing **only with a good motive** — the desire to help the public.

**When is whistleblowing ethically justified?**

The following conditions are generally considered necessary:

1. **Serious harm:** The employee's organisation is involved in an action that could cause serious harm to the public (or employees).
2. **Internal reporting:** The employee has reported their concern to their immediate supervisor and is convinced that the supervisor is not going to resolve it.
3. **Exhausted internal channels:** The employee has tried and failed all internal channels — up the reporting chain, to ethics hotlines, etc.
4. **Evidence:** The employee has documented evidence that would convince a reasonable person that the concern is justified.
5. **Good faith belief:** The employee genuinely believes that exposing the wrongdoing will prevent the harm.

**Whistleblower: Hero or Traitor?**
Some argue that whistleblowing is always a betrayal of loyalty to one's employer. Others argue that the duty to the public overrides loyalty to the organisation. The dominant view in professional ethics is that properly motivated, well-documented whistleblowing is both ethically justified and sometimes obligatory.

---

### 4.4 Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) and Business Ethics

**Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR):** The concept that an organisation should act ethically by taking responsibility for the impact of its actions on the environment, the community, and the welfare of its employees.

**Business Ethics:** Refers to the principles, values, and moral guidelines that guide the conduct of individuals and organisations in the business context.

**Elements of an ethical workplace culture:**
- Clear ethical codes and standards.
- Training programs for employees.
- Confidential reporting mechanisms (ethics hotlines).
- Consistent enforcement.
- Importance of Leadership Commitment: When leaders consistently demonstrate and prioritise ethical behaviour, it sets a powerful example for employees throughout the organisation. Leaders should communicate the importance of ethics, allocate resources, and actively support and promote ethics programs.

## Lecture 5 — Privacy and Data Protection {#lecture-5}

### 5.1 What is Privacy?

**Privacy** is a value that relates to the right of people to control information about themselves, and to be let alone.

**The Balance:** Privacy must be balanced against competing values such as:
- Transparency
- Accountability
- Public safety

This requires judgement — there is no single correct answer for all situations.

---

### 5.2 Why Privacy Matters

**Arguments for Privacy:**
- Privacy enables individuals to develop their identities and personalities without external interference.
- Privacy protects individuals from exploitation and manipulation.
- Privacy is necessary for freedom — the ability to act without constant surveillance or social pressure.

---

### 5.3 Threats to Privacy

Modern technology has created significant new threats to privacy:
- **Data collection and aggregation** — large amounts of personal data collected by organisations.
- **Surveillance** — CCTV, location tracking, monitoring of digital communications.
- **Data breaches** — unauthorised access to personal information.
- **Social media** — voluntary sharing of personal information that can be used in unexpected ways.

**Examples of Misuse of Personal Data:**
- Unsolicited marketing without consent.
- Sharing of personal data to other parties without consent.
- Location tracking without consent.

---

### 5.4 Personal Data

**Personal data** refers to any information that relates to an identified or identifiable individual.

**Personally Identifiable Information (PII)** specifically refers to data that can be used to identify or directly link to an individual. Typically includes:
- Full name
- NRIC, FIN, or passport number
- Driver's licence number
- Personal email address or mobile phone number
- Photos

---

### 5.5 Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA)

Singapore's **Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA)** governs the collection, use, disclosure, and care of personal data by organisations.

**Key PDPA Obligations:**

**Consent Obligation:** Requires organisations to obtain consent from individuals before collecting, using, or disclosing their personal data. Consent must be:
- Informed
- Specific
- Given voluntarily

**Notification Obligation:** Requires organisations to inform individuals of the purposes for collecting, using, or disclosing their personal data, as well as the organisations or types of organisations to which their data may be disclosed.

**Purpose Limitation Obligation:** Requires organisations to collect, use, or disclose personal data only for purposes that have been notified to individuals and for which they have obtained consent. *Note: If organisations wish to use existing personal data for a new purpose, consent for the new purpose will need to be sought.*

**Additional key obligations include:**
- **Access and Correction** — Individuals have the right to access and correct their personal data.
- **Accuracy** — Organisations should make reasonable efforts to ensure personal data is accurate and complete.
- **Protection** — Reasonable security arrangements must be made to protect personal data from unauthorised access, disclosure, copying, use, modification, or disposal.
- **Retention Limitation** — Personal data should not be retained longer than necessary for the purposes for which it was collected.
- **Transfer Limitation** — Personal data must not be transferred outside Singapore unless adequate protections are in place.

---

### 5.6 Ethical Frameworks Applied to Privacy

**Kantianism:** Privacy should be respected because treating people merely as data points (means to an end) — without their knowledge or consent — violates the categorical imperative. People have a right to control information about themselves.

**Utilitarianism:** Privacy protections may be justified if, on balance, they produce greater well-being by protecting individuals from exploitation, even though some societal benefits (e.g., better targeted public health interventions) may be foregone.

**Social Contract Theory:** Individuals in society would agree to privacy protections as part of the social contract, because they benefit everyone — no one wants their private information misused.

**Virtue Ethics:** Respecting privacy is a matter of honesty, respect, and integrity — virtues that should guide IT professionals in handling personal data.

## Lecture 7 — Privacy and the Government {#lecture-7}

### 7.1 Government and Privacy

Governments collect vast amounts of personal data from citizens. Types of government-held personal data include:
- Birth certificates, national ID cards, marriage certificates.
- Motor vehicle records.
- HR records of public employees.
- Tax returns.
- Education and qualifications.
- Immigration and travel data.
- Social benefits and welfare data.
- Criminal records, deeds to property, health data.

---

### 7.2 The Privacy vs. Security Tension

The terrorist attacks of September 11, 2001, dramatically shifted public discourse on the balance between privacy and national security. Governments introduced sweeping new surveillance powers in the name of counterterrorism.

**The tension:**
- **Security argument:** Some invasion of privacy may be justified to prevent serious harm (terrorism, crime). The potential harm (mass casualties) is so grave that it justifies intrusions.
- **Privacy argument:** Mass surveillance infringes on civil liberties, can be abused, chills free expression, and may not be effective at preventing terrorism.

---

### 7.3 Culture and Privacy

Privacy expectations are shaped by culture.

| **Individualistic Culture** | **Collectivist Culture** |
|---|---|
| Personal-emphasised | Social-emphasised |
| Views people as unique | Views people as part of a group |
| Encourages self-expression | Discourages self-expression |
| Emphasises competitiveness | Emphasises group harmony |

- Individualistic cultures (e.g., USA, Northern Europe) tend to place higher value on individual privacy.
- Collectivist cultures (e.g., many Asian societies) may accept more state access to personal information in the interest of the collective good.

---

### 7.4 Government Surveillance — Ethical Analysis

**Supporting government surveillance (Utilitarian arguments):**
- If surveillance prevents terrorist attacks or serious crime, the benefit (lives saved, harm prevented) may outweigh the cost (privacy lost).
- Most law-abiding citizens rarely feel the direct intrusion.

**Opposing government surveillance:**
- **Kantian argument:** Using citizens as instruments of the state — collecting their data without consent — treats them merely as means, violating their dignity.
- **Social Contract argument:** Citizens would not agree to comprehensive surveillance if they could see the full terms. It undermines the trust that the social contract depends on.
- Surveillance can be abused; authoritarian regimes can use surveillance infrastructure to oppress.
- The "chilling effect" — knowledge of surveillance causes people to self-censor, suppressing free speech and association.

## Lecture 8 — Information Security {#lecture-8}

### 8.1 What is Information Security?

**Information security** is the practice of protecting information systems and the data they contain from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.

**The CIA Triad — Three Goals of Information Security:**
- **Confidentiality:** Ensuring information is accessible only to those authorised to access it.
- **Integrity:** Safeguarding the accuracy and completeness of information and processing methods.
- **Availability:** Ensuring that authorised users have access to information and associated assets when required.

**Some Threats to Information Security:**
- Malware (viruses, worms, ransomware, trojans)
- Vulnerabilities in software and systems
- Unsecured wireless networks
- Social engineering (phishing, pretexting)
- Insider threats

---

### 8.2 Types of Malware

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Virus** | A program that attaches itself to a legitimate file and spreads when the file is executed. |
| **Worm** | A self-replicating program that spreads across networks without needing to attach to a file. |
| **Trojan horse** | A program that appears legitimate but contains malicious code. |
| **Ransomware** | Malware that encrypts a victim's files and demands payment for the decryption key. |
| **Spyware** | Software that secretly monitors user activity and transmits information to a third party. |
| **Adware** | Software that automatically displays or downloads advertising material. |

---

### 8.3 Hacker Ethics

**The Hacker Ethic** is a set of beliefs that originated in the hacking community, based on principles such as:
- The belief that information should be free and accessible.
- Sharing — information, code, and knowledge should be shared widely.
- Decentralisation — computers should be decentralised tools for freedom.

**Conflict between Hacker Ethics and Professional Code of Ethics:**

Some hacker tenets directly conflict with professional codes of ethics:

*Example 1 (Trespass):* In line with the hacker ethic, a hacker may break into a system to expose vulnerabilities. However, this violates ACM 2.8: "Access computing and communication resources only when authorised or when compelled by the public good."

*Example 2 (Sharing):* In line with the sharing tenet, a hacker may share copyrighted materials on P2P networks so that more people can benefit from shared intellectual properties. However, this violates ACM 1.5: "Respect the work required to produce new ideas, inventions, creative works."

**Conclusion:** While hacker ethics contains some admirable ideals (open access, sharing knowledge), when applied without regard to consent, law, or property rights, it conflicts with professional ethical standards.

---

### 8.4 Ethical Frameworks Applied to Information Security

**Kantian analysis of hacking:** Hacking into someone's system without authorisation uses the system owner as a means to an end (gathering information, proving a point) without their consent. This violates the 2nd formulation of the categorical imperative.

**Utilitarian analysis of hacking:** Could there be cases where hacking produces net good? (E.g., a security researcher who hacks a system to expose vulnerabilities before a malicious actor does.) Act utilitarianism might permit this if the benefits outweigh the harms. However, rule utilitarianism suggests that a rule permitting unauthorised access would, if followed universally, produce net harm.

## Lecture 9 — Intellectual Property {#lecture-9}

### 9.1 What is Intellectual Property?

**Intellectual Property (IP)** refers to creations of the mind — inventions, literary and artistic works, designs, and symbols, names and images used in commerce.

IP is a form of **intangible asset** — an asset that is non-physical in nature.

**Copyright is automatically granted upon creation** (no registration required).

---

### 9.2 Types of IP Protection

| Type | Description | Registrable? | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Copyright** | Protects original creative works (literature, music, software, films). | No — automatic | Life of author + 70 years (varies) |
| **Patent** | Protects inventions and innovations — gives the holder exclusive rights to use, make, or sell. | Yes | ~20 years |
| **Trademark** | Protects brand identifiers — names, logos, slogans. | Yes | Renewable indefinitely |
| **Trade Secret** | Confidential and proprietary information providing competitive advantage. | No (protected via NDA/contractual means) | No time limit |
| **Designs** | Protects the visual appearance of a product. | Yes | Varies |
| **Geographical Indication** | Protects names/signs used on products from a specific place. | Yes | Varies |

**Trade Secrets — Computing Examples:**
- Proprietary algorithms
- Formulas
- Source code
- Customer lists
- Sensitive business information

Characteristics: Not registrable; there is no time limit as long as secrecy is maintained.

---

### 9.3 Copyright in the Digital Age

The digital age has made copyright infringement dramatically easier:
- Perfect copies can be made at near-zero cost.
- Files can be distributed globally instantly via P2P networks and the internet.

**Software Piracy:** The unauthorised copying, distribution, or use of software. Software is protected by copyright — the licence agreement specifies the terms of use.

**Open Source Software:** Software released with a licence that allows users to view, modify, and distribute the source code. Different from public domain (no copyright) — open source software still has copyright but is licensed permissively.

**Fair Use / Fair Dealing:** Limited use of copyrighted material without permission for purposes such as education, commentary, criticism, news reporting, and research. Whether something constitutes fair use depends on factors such as:
- The purpose and character of the use.
- The nature of the copyrighted work.
- The amount and substantiality of the portion used.
- The effect of the use on the market for the original work.

---

### 9.4 Ethical Analysis of IP Issues

**Why should IP be protected?**
- **Kantian argument:** IP reflects the creative work of real people. Using their work without permission treats them merely as a means (a source of free content) without their consent — violating the 2nd formulation.
- **Utilitarian argument:** IP protection incentivises innovation and creation by ensuring creators can benefit financially from their work. Without such incentives, fewer creative works and innovations would be produced, reducing overall societal benefit.
- **Social Contract argument:** Society has collectively agreed (through law and social norms) to respect IP rights because the system benefits everyone — creators get rewarded, and society gets innovation.

**Arguments against strong IP protection:**
- Overly broad IP protection can restrict the free flow of information and impede further innovation.
- In some cases (e.g., pharmaceutical patents in developing countries), strict enforcement can cause significant harm.

## Lecture 10 — Work and Wealth {#lecture-10}

### 10.1 IT and Automation

Advances in IT have dramatically changed the nature of work through **automation** — the use of technology to perform tasks previously done by humans.

**Historical context:** Automation is not new. The industrial revolution mechanised physical labour. The information revolution is now automating cognitive tasks as well.

**Effects of automation on employment:**
- Some jobs are eliminated as machines can perform them more cheaply.
- New jobs are created in designing, building, and maintaining automated systems.
- The nature of remaining jobs changes — often requiring higher-level skills.

---

### 10.2 The Digital Divide

The **digital divide** refers to the gap between people who have effective access to digital and information technology, and those who have limited or no access.

**Dimensions of the digital divide:**
- Access to devices (computers, smartphones).
- Access to internet connectivity.
- Digital literacy — the ability to use technology effectively.

**Who is affected?**
- Geographic divide (urban vs. rural).
- Economic divide (wealthy vs. poor).
- Age divide (younger vs. older generations).
- Global divide (developed vs. developing nations).

**Ethical implications:** The digital divide raises concerns about fairness and equality of opportunity — those without access to digital technology are increasingly disadvantaged in education, employment, and civic participation.

---

### 10.3 Globalisation and Outsourcing

IT has enabled **globalisation** — the integration of economies and societies across the world — and has made it much easier to **outsource** work to lower-cost locations.

**Arguments for outsourcing:**
- Reduces costs, making goods and services cheaper.
- Allows companies to focus on their core competencies.
- Provides employment opportunities in developing countries.

**Arguments against outsourcing:**
- Displaces workers in higher-cost countries.
- Can lead to exploitation of workers in countries with weaker labour protections.
- May undermine local communities.

---

### 10.4 IT Professional Responsibility in the Workplace

The SCS Code of Conduct states that IT professionals should continue to upgrade their knowledge and skills and be aware of relevant developments in the technology they are involved in.

**Key professional duties in the workplace:**
- Maintain and continuously develop technical competence.
- Apply skills ethically in the workplace — not just effectively.
- Balance loyalty to employers with broader obligations to clients, users, and the public.
- Raise concerns about unethical practices through appropriate channels.

## Lecture 11 — AI Ethics {#lecture-11}

### 11.1 Categorisation of AI

**Weak AI (Narrow AI):** AI designed and trained for a specific task. It does not have general reasoning abilities beyond its specific domain.
- *Examples:* Speech recognition (Siri, Alexa), image classification, recommendation systems.

**Strong AI (General AI / AGI):** Hypothetical AI that has the ability to perform any intellectual task that a human can. It has general reasoning abilities. Does not yet exist.

**Superintelligence:** Hypothetical AI that surpasses human intelligence in virtually all domains. A subject of significant ethical debate.

---

### 11.2 Ethical Concerns of AI Use

#### 11.2.1 Bias and Fairness

AI systems can exhibit and even amplify biases present in training data.

*Examples:*
- Facial recognition systems with higher error rates for certain demographic groups.
- Hiring algorithms that discriminate against certain groups.
- Criminal recidivism tools that are biased against minority defendants.

**Why this is a problem:** Biased AI systems can have real-world consequences — denied loans, wrongful imprisonment, missed medical diagnoses.

**Ethical analysis:**
- **Kantian:** Using a biased AI system treats affected individuals as less worthy of accurate assessment — a failure to respect persons equally.
- **Utilitarian:** Biased AI may increase overall unhappiness by producing unjust outcomes.
- **Social Contract:** People would not agree to systems that systematically disadvantage them.

#### 11.2.2 Misuse

Like other technologies, AI can be misused.

*Examples:*
- **Deepfakes:** AI-generated synthetic media (images, video, audio) that can be used to impersonate people — e.g., scammers using deepfake technology to impersonate relatives and trick victims into transferring money.
- **Disinformation:** AI-generated content used to spread false information at scale.
- **Surveillance:** AI-powered surveillance systems used by authoritarian governments to monitor and control populations.
- **Autonomous weapons:** AI systems that can select and engage targets without human intervention.

#### 11.2.3 Accountability and Transparency

When an AI system causes harm, who is responsible?
- The developer of the AI model?
- The organisation that deployed it?
- The individual who used it?

The **opacity** ("black box" nature) of many AI systems — especially deep learning — makes it difficult to understand why a particular decision was made, complicating accountability.

**Explainability:** The ability to explain in human-understandable terms why an AI system made a particular decision. Increasingly seen as an ethical requirement, especially in high-stakes domains (healthcare, criminal justice, lending).

#### 11.2.4 Privacy

AI systems often require large amounts of personal data to function. This raises significant privacy concerns:
- Data collection and use that individuals may not be aware of or have not consented to.
- Inference attacks — using AI to infer sensitive attributes from seemingly innocuous data.
- Surveillance using computer vision and other AI capabilities.

#### 11.2.5 Job Displacement

AI-powered automation threatens to displace significant numbers of workers across many industries.

**Counter-argument:** Technology has always created new types of jobs while eliminating old ones. The long-term net effect on employment may be positive.

**Concern:** The transition may be disruptive — workers displaced by AI may not have the skills required for the new jobs being created, leading to structural unemployment and increased inequality.

---

### 11.3 Principles for Ethical AI

Several organisations and governments have proposed principles for ethical AI. Common themes include:

1. **Fairness** — AI should treat people fairly and without discrimination.
2. **Transparency** — People should be able to understand how AI systems make decisions.
3. **Accountability** — Clear lines of responsibility must exist for AI systems.
4. **Privacy** — AI systems must respect individuals' privacy rights.
5. **Safety and Security** — AI systems must be safe and secure.
6. **Human oversight** — Humans should remain in control of AI systems, especially in high-stakes applications.

---

### 11.4 Ethical Frameworks Applied to AI

**Kantianism:**
- AI systems that make decisions affecting individuals without transparency or the ability to appeal treat those individuals merely as means — as objects to be processed rather than as rational agents with rights.
- AI systems should be designed to respect human autonomy and dignity.

**Utilitarianism:**
- AI can produce enormous social benefits — improved healthcare diagnoses, more efficient resource allocation, personalised education.
- But if AI harms are concentrated on vulnerable groups, the utilitarian calculus may not justify deployment.
- Act utilitarian analysis should consider all affected parties, including those who bear disproportionate risks.

**Social Contract Theory:**
- People would agree to AI use if it is subject to proper oversight, accountability, and fairness.
- People would not agree to AI systems that systematically disadvantage them or that operate without transparency.

**Virtue Ethics:**
- AI developers and deployers should cultivate virtues of honesty, responsibility, and care in designing and deploying AI systems.
- Asks: "What would a responsible, virtuous engineer/organisation do?"
