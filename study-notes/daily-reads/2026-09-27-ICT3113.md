## Response Time, Percentiles and the Tail

Response time R decomposes cleanly into R = W + S: wait time W (queued, blocked, or otherwise not being actively processed) plus service time S (the server is actively working on this specific request, and nothing else). The classification rule is simple once stated plainly: if the server is actively doing your work right now, it's service time; every other moment — including being distracted, or waiting for someone else — is wait time. In the "Larry the Procrastinator" example, going to the washroom and setting up his laptop before starting the lab counts as wait time, not service — only the minutes actually spent working on the lab count as S.

A worked polyclinic example makes the split concrete: 25 minutes queuing before registration (W) + 10 minutes with the doctor (S) + 15 minutes waiting for test results (W) + 5 minutes for a follow-up procedure (S) gives R = 55 minutes total, with S = 15 min and W = 40 min — 73% of the visit is pure waiting. The notes add a non-obvious insight here: reducing the doctor's service time by even a few minutes per patient saves more than just those minutes for one patient — every patient queued behind them also enters service earlier, so the total time saved across all patients exceeds the per-patient cut.

Once you have a distribution of response times rather than a single value, percentiles matter more than the mean. Real-world response time distributions are right-skewed — a long tail of slow outliers pulls the mean upward and hides how most users actually experience the system. That is why SLOs are set on tail percentiles (commonly p95 or p99) rather than the mean: a p95 target of 120 ms is a concrete, contractable commitment that 95% of users get a response within that time. User-perception research gives rough thresholds: around 0.1 s feels instantaneous, around 1 s the delay is noticed but the flow of thought is maintained, and around 10 s a progress indicator becomes necessary.

> [!warning] Watch out
> An **SLI** is the metric being measured, an **SLO** is the target value for that metric, and an **SLA** is the formal agreement built around the SLO — students regularly swap SLI and SLO in exam answers.

## Multi-Server Queues (M/M/c)

M/M/c extends the single-server M/M/1 model to c identical servers sharing one queue — the standard model for a load balancer in front of several application servers, or a thread pool with c workers. The stability condition generalises directly: utilisation ρ = λ/(c×μ), and the system is only stable when ρ < 1, i.e. when arrival rate λ stays below the combined service rate c×μ.

A subtle, exam-favourite result: given the same total aggregate capacity, one very fast server (rate c×μ) always produces lower latency than c slower servers (each rate μ), because in the single-fast-server case every job gets the full combined rate to itself, whereas in the multi-server case each job is limited to just one server's rate μ. Despite this, real systems overwhelmingly choose many servers — not because queueing theory favours it, but for operational reasons: a single device cannot scale indefinitely, and with c servers, one failure only removes 1/c of total capacity rather than taking the whole system down. A typical real-world example is three API servers behind one load balancer, all drawing from a single shared queue.

A worked API-tier example shows the stakes: with λ = 8 tps, S = 148 ms and c = 3 servers, normal operation gives ρ ≈ 0.40 per server and a mean wait of roughly 7 ms — but if one server goes down (c drops to 2), ρ jumps to 0.60 and the mean wait balloons to roughly 58 ms, an eightfold degradation from losing just a third of the capacity.

> [!warning] Watch out
> "More servers is always better for latency" is false — the fault-tolerance argument, not a latency argument, is why multi-server designs are preferred in practice.

## Operational Laws — Forced Flow and Service Demand

Operational laws relate throughput, visits, and utilisation across the devices (tiers) inside a system, using only measurable quantities — no distributional assumptions required.

The Forced Flow Law states X_k = V_k × X: the throughput at any device k is forced into fixed proportion to overall system throughput X by that device's visit ratio V_k (how many times, on average, one system-level transaction visits device k). Worked example: if every web transaction issues V = 4 SQL queries and system throughput is X = 25 tps, the database must be handling X_db = 4 × 25 = 100 queries per second — no direct measurement of the database required.

Service Demand D_k = V_k × S_k captures the total service time one transaction requires at device k, summed across all its visits. Worked example: if V = 4 queries and each takes S = 5 ms, the database's service demand is D_db = 4 × 5 = 20 ms; at a throughput of X = 30 tps, this gives a database utilisation of U_db = 30 × 0.020 = 60%. Combined with the Utilisation Law, this gives the Service Demand Law: U_k = X × D_k — or, rearranged, D_k = U_k / X, the practically important direction: it lets you compute a device's service demand purely from monitoring data (utilisation and throughput), without tracing individual transactions.

A worked running-case table shows why this matters diagnostically: API tier, database, and storage devices can all show moderate, unsaturated utilisation while the system still exhibits terrible tail latency (p95 = 3.5 s). When forced-flow and service-demand accounting rule out capacity as the cause, the next place to look is variability (Kingman formula territory), not demand or capacity.

> [!warning] Watch out
> Forced Flow and the Service Demand Law are the two formulas examiners most often chain together in one multi-step question — compute V_k first, then D_k, then U_k or X_k.
