## Diffie–Hellman Key Exchange

Diffie–Hellman (DH) solves a problem symmetric cryptography cannot solve on its own: how do two parties agree on a shared secret key over a channel an eavesdropper can freely observe, without ever transmitting the key itself? Crucially, DH is **not** encryption — it is purely a key-agreement protocol; the shared secret it produces is then used with a symmetric cipher to actually encrypt data.

The mechanism: Alice and Bob first publicly agree on a large prime p and a generator G — these can be shared in the open. Each then picks a private secret (a for Alice, b for Bob) that is never transmitted, and sends the other a public value: A = Gᵃ mod p and B = Gᵇ mod p. Alice computes K = Bᵃ mod p, and Bob computes K = Aᵇ mod p — both arrive at the identical value K = G^(ab) mod p, without either ever sending a, b, or K itself.

An eavesdropper who intercepts everything transmitted — p, G, A, and B — still cannot recover K, because doing so requires solving the **discrete logarithm problem** (recovering a from A = Gᵃ mod p), which is computationally infeasible for suitably large primes.

> [!warning] Watch out
> Students often confuse DH with RSA. RSA is a full public-key encryption/signature scheme built on the **factoring problem**; DH is a key-agreement protocol built on the **discrete logarithm problem**, and on its own it encrypts nothing.

## Authentication Factors

Access control follows a three-stage pipeline: identification (who are you claiming to be?), authentication (prove it), and authorisation (what are you allowed to do?). Authentication itself rests on three independent factor categories, remembered with the mnemonic **KTB**: Knowledge (something you know — a password, PIN, or passphrase), Token (something you have — a smart card, eToken, or RFID tag), and Biometrics (something you are, covered in the next section). Multi-factor authentication (MFA/2FA) combines two or more of these categories, and is significantly stronger than any single factor, because compromising one factor type does not automatically compromise the others.

Password security follows a progressive-hardening ladder. Storing plaintext passwords means one breach exposes everything; storing them encrypted is reversible if the key leaks; storing a plain hash is vulnerable to precomputed rainbow-table lookups; storing Hash(password + salt) is best practice — a unique random salt per user means an attacker must crack every password individually, since no precomputed table can cover every possible salt value.

Common password attacks include brute force (try all combinations — defend with length and account-lockout policies), dictionary attacks (try common words and variants — defend by avoiding dictionary words and using salted hashing), rainbow tables (precomputed hash-to-password lookup — salting renders these useless), social engineering (mining personal details from social media), and Google dorking (search operators such as ext:csv inurl:passwd to find exposed credential files left publicly indexed).

Token authentication covers eTokens, smart cards, and RFID tags — physical devices that store keys or certificates. RFID in particular carries specific vulnerabilities: over-the-air spoofing, man-in-the-middle sniffing, and side-channel power analysis, since many RFID tags are passive and reader-powered with limited onboard security.

> [!warning] Watch out
> Knowing which factor category a given mechanism belongs to (Knowledge / Token / Biometric) is a frequent single-mark exam question — classify by what the user needs to know, have, or be.

## Biometric Authentication

Biometric authentication verifies identity using physical or behavioural characteristics, and a good biometric must satisfy four properties, remembered as **UDPC**: universality (everyone must have the characteristic), distinctiveness (any two people must differ enough to be told apart), permanence (the characteristic must remain stable over a person's lifetime), and collectability (it must be practically and quantitatively measurable).

Accuracy is judged with two complementary rates: the False Accept Rate (FAR) — how often an impostor is wrongly granted access, ideally 0 — and the True Accept Rate (TAR) — how often the legitimate user is correctly granted access, ideally 1. These two move together rather than in opposite directions: loosening a system's match threshold to admit more legitimate users almost always raises both TAR and FAR simultaneously, since a looser threshold is simply more permissive overall. The ROC curve plots this trade-off across every possible threshold setting.

Different modalities trade off accuracy, speed, cost, and invasiveness. Retinal scanning is the most accurate (roughly 1-in-20,000 false-positive rate) but also the most invasive and expensive; iris recognition is nearly as accurate and far less invasive; fingerprint recognition is fast and cheap but affected by dryness or injury; facial recognition is fast and increasingly accurate with machine learning, but vulnerable to spoofing; gait and keystroke dynamics are continuous, low-overhead behavioural modalities with more moderate accuracy.

> [!warning] Watch out
> Facial recognition carries a well-documented fairness problem — false-positive rates for Asian and Black individuals can run up to 100× higher than for white individuals, a point examiners increasingly test directly.
